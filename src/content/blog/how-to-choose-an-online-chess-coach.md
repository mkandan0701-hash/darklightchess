**Short answer:** the best online chess class for your child has a live coach (not just videos), a placement assessment, a structured level-by-level curriculum, a coach whose playing record you can check, small groups or one-to-one lessons, and class times that fit your time zone. A free trial lesson is the easiest way to check all of this before you pay.

## 1. Is the class live?

Recorded videos and apps are useful for practice, but a child improves fastest when a coach watches them think and corrects mistakes as they happen. Ask whether every lesson is live and how many children are in a class.

## 2. Can you check the coach's record?

You don't need a grandmaster for a beginner, but you should be able to see that the coach can really play and has taught children. Ways to check:

- **FIDE rating:** search the coach's name on [ratings.fide.com](https://ratings.fide.com/) to see their international rating and title, if they have one.
- **US Chess or CFC record:** both federations have public player lookups that show ratings and past events.
- **Teaching experience:** ask how long they have taught children of your child's age, and what students they have taken to tournaments.

A strong player isn't automatically a good teacher of seven-year-olds. Look for both.

## 3. Is there a curriculum?

Ask what your child will learn in the first three months and how they move up. A good academy can show you its levels and what each one covers. At Darklight, children move through five levels, from complete beginner to 1800+ tournament players. See [our courses](/courses/).

## 4. Does the trial lesson include an assessment?

A useful first lesson isn't a sales pitch. The coach should play and solve positions with your child, tell you their level, and suggest a class. If the trial feels like a generic presentation, that tells you something.

## 5. Do the class times work for your family?

For families in the US and Canada learning with coaches abroad, check that classes run after school in your own time zone and not late at night. Ask for the time in your zone, not theirs. Our US and Canadian classes run between about 4 and 8 pm local time on weekdays, plus weekend mornings.

## 6. How is progress reported?

Ask how you will know your child is improving: a short note after each level, a game review, or results in real games. Ratings move slowly, so look for coaches who explain progress in plain language.

## 7. What does it cost, and what's included?

Compare the price per hour, not per package, and check the currency. Our guides to chess lesson costs in the [US](/blog/how-much-do-chess-lessons-cost-in-the-us/) and [Canada](/blog/how-much-do-chess-lessons-cost-in-canada/) list typical prices.

## Red flags

- Promises of a specific rating, title or school result.
- No way to check who will teach your child.
- No assessment, so every child is put in the same class.
- Recorded-only lessons sold as "coaching".
- Pressure to buy a long package before the first real lesson.

## Questions to ask before you sign up

1. Who will teach my child, and how can I check their playing record?
2. How many children are in a class?
3. What will my child learn in the first three months?
4. How will you tell me about progress?
5. What happens if a class is missed?
6. Can siblings learn together?
