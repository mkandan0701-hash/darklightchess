Every chess player, no matter how strong, started at the same place: learning what the pieces do and how a game is won, lost or drawn. This guide covers the actual rules of chess in plain language, without assuming you know any of it already. Once these are second nature, you're ready to think about strategy rather than legality.

## How Each Piece Moves

- **Pawn** — moves straight ahead one square, or two squares on its very first move. It captures diagonally, one square forward, never straight ahead. When a pawn reaches the far end of the board, it must promote — usually to a queen, though a knight, rook or bishop are also legal choices.
- **Knight** — moves in an "L" shape: two squares in one direction, then one square at a right angle. It's the only piece that can jump over others.
- **Bishop** — moves any number of squares diagonally, and stays on the same colour square for the whole game. Each side has one light-squared and one dark-squared bishop.
- **Rook** — moves any number of squares along a rank (row) or file (column).
- **Queen** — combines the rook and bishop's movement, making it the most powerful piece on the board.
- **King** — moves one square in any direction. It's the piece you must protect at all costs, since losing it means losing the game.

## Check, Checkmate and Stalemate

A king is in **check** when it's under direct attack. When that happens, you must get out of check immediately — by moving the king to safety, blocking the attack with another piece, or capturing the attacking piece. You are never allowed to make a move that leaves your own king in check.

**Checkmate** happens when a king is in check and there is no legal way to escape it. This ends the game immediately — whoever delivers checkmate wins.

**Stalemate** is different and often confuses new players: it's when the player to move has no legal move at all, but their king is *not* in check. A stalemate is a draw, not a loss, even if one side has far more material. Learning to spot stalemate traps — and avoid handing your opponent one when you're winning — is one of the first sharp skills a beginner should build.

## Special Moves Worth Knowing

### Castling

Castling lets you move your king two squares toward a rook, with the rook jumping to the square beside the king, in a single move. It's the only time two pieces move at once, and it's usually the fastest way to get your king to safety while connecting your rooks. Castling is only legal if:

- Neither the king nor that rook has moved yet in the game
- There are no pieces between them
- The king is not currently in check
- The king doesn't pass through or land on a square attacked by an enemy piece

### En Passant

This is the rule beginners find hardest to believe the first time they see it. If an enemy pawn moves two squares forward from its starting position and lands beside one of your pawns, you may capture it as though it had only moved one square — but only on your very next move. Miss that window, and the option disappears.

### Pawn Promotion

As mentioned above, a pawn that reaches the eighth rank (or first rank, for Black) must turn into another piece — almost always a queen, since it's the strongest. Occasionally a stronger player will "underpromote" to a knight instead, when a knight's move delivers checkmate or avoids a stalemate that a queen would cause.

## How a Game Can End in a Draw

Besides stalemate, a game is drawn when:

- The same exact position occurs three times (**threefold repetition**)
- Fifty moves pass with no pawn move and no capture (**the fifty-move rule**)
- Neither side has enough material left to force checkmate, such as king and bishop versus a lone king (**insufficient material**)
- Both players agree to a draw

Knowing these rules matters even at club level — plenty of games are thrown away by a player who doesn't realise their winning position has quietly become a stalemate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need to say "check" out loud when I attack the king?

No, it isn't required by the rules — it's just a courtesy, especially with beginners. In tournament play, most players stay silent and simply make the move.

### Can a king ever capture a piece?

Yes, as long as the square the piece is on isn't defended by another enemy piece — capturing into check is illegal, like any other move that leaves your king in check.

### What happens if I touch a piece by mistake?

In casual games, nothing — just be careful and move on. In rated tournaments, the touch-move rule usually applies: if a piece can legally move, touching it means you must move it.
