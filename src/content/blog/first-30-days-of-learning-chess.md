**Short answer:** in the first 30 days, a beginner should learn how every piece moves, what check and checkmate mean, and how to play short games, then start full games by week four. Keep practice to 10 to 20 minutes a day, a few days a week. The goal for month one is enjoying the game and playing legally, not winning.

## Week 1: The pieces

- Learn one or two pieces per session: how they move and how they capture. Start with the rook and bishop, which move in straight lines, then the queen, king, knight and pawns.
- Play **mini-games** with only a few pieces, such as "pawn wars" (each side has only pawns; first to reach the far side wins).
- Practice: 10 minutes a day, three or four days.

**Sign it's going well:** the child can set up the board and show how each piece moves.

## Week 2: Check, checkmate and the king

- What check means and the three ways out of it: move the king, block, or capture the attacker.
- Checkmate with simple patterns, such as two rooks against a king.
- Stalemate, so a winning position isn't thrown away.

**Sign it's going well:** the child recognizes check without being told.

## Week 3: Special moves and first full games

- Castling, pawn promotion and en passant.
- First full games against a parent or coach, with help allowed.
- One rule that saves many games: before each move, check whether any of your pieces can be captured.

**Sign it's going well:** the child finishes a game without illegal moves.

## Week 4: Simple tactics and opening habits

- Forks (one piece attacking two) and capturing undefended pieces.
- Three opening principles: control the center, bring out knights and bishops, castle early. Our guide to [5 opening principles every beginner should master](/blog/5-opening-principles-every-beginner-should-master/) explains them.
- Two or three full games a week.

**Sign it's going well:** the child wants to play again after losing.

## How much should a beginner practice?

Little and often works best. Ten to 20 minutes on three or four days beats one long session at the weekend. Mix puzzles, mini-games and one full game. For a fuller routine by age, see [how often kids should practice chess](/blog/how-often-should-kids-practice-chess/).

## What if my child gets frustrated?

Frustration is normal in the first month, especially after losing. Play shorter games, let them replay a move after a blunder while they're learning, and praise good thinking rather than results. If frustration continues, a coach can adjust the pace; our [young kids' classes](/chess-classes-for-young-kids/) are built for ages 4 to 7.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should my child use a chess app in the first month?
An app is fine for puzzles and practice games, but keep the first weeks mostly on a real board or with a person, so the child learns the rules properly and enjoys playing with others.

### When should a beginner start lessons with a coach?
Any time. A coach helps most in the first months, when bad habits are easiest to prevent.

### How do I know if chess is right for my child?
If they ask to play again, enjoy puzzles or talk about their games, it's a good sign. Give it at least a month before deciding.
