Watch enough beginner games and you'll notice the same handful of mistakes appearing again and again, regardless of who's playing. None of them require talent to fix — just awareness. Here are the ones we see most often in our own classes, and what to do instead of each.

## Bringing the Queen Out Too Early

New players often love moving the queen on move two or three because it feels powerful and threatens things immediately. The problem is that your opponent develops a piece while chasing your queen away, gaining time for free. A good rule of thumb: bring your knights and bishops out first, and let your queen join the game once there's a real square worth going to.

## Skipping Castling

A king left in the centre is comfortable until it suddenly isn't — one open file or diagonal and the game can end in a handful of moves. Beginners often delay castling because "nothing is attacking yet," but the whole point of castling early is to be safe *before* an attack starts, not after. If you notice you're on move twelve and still haven't castled, stop and ask why.

## Chasing a Single Piece Around the Board

It's tempting to hunt down an enemy knight or bishop that seems to be in your way, but doing so with your own undeveloped pieces usually costs several tempi while the rest of your army stays at home. Unless capturing that piece is genuinely worth the time spent, leave it alone and keep developing.

## Weakening Pawns Around Your Own King

Moves like an unnecessary g4 or h4 near your castled king might look aggressive, but they also open lines and squares your opponent can use to attack you later. Every pawn move in front of your king is one less piece of shelter — make sure there's a concrete reason before you push one.

## Not Checking for Hanging Pieces

This is the single biggest reason beginner games are decided early: a piece left undefended, or moved to a square another piece already attacks. Before every move, get in the habit of asking three questions — what did my opponent's last move threaten, is anything of mine currently undefended, and does my move leave anything hanging? This one habit alone prevents more losses than any opening knowledge could. ## Trading Pieces Without a Reason

Not every trade is good just because it's available. Ask what the trade achieves — does it improve your position, remove an active enemy piece, or simplify toward an ending you're winning? Trading simply because a piece "can" be captured, especially your own well-placed pieces for a passive enemy piece, often hands your opponent the better position for free.

## Playing Too Fast

Especially online, it's easy to move almost as fast as you can click. But most blunders happen in the two or three seconds it takes to glance at a move without really checking it. Slowing down — even by five or ten seconds per move in a casual game — catches a surprising number of mistakes before they happen.

## How to Actually Fix These

Knowing about a mistake and not making it under pressure are two different skills. The fastest way to close that gap is to review your own games afterwards and look specifically for which of these patterns showed up, rather than just checking whether you won or lost. Pairing that habit with a solid grasp of [basic chess rules](/blog/chess-rules-for-beginners/) and a simple, principled [opening](/blog/best-chess-openings-for-beginners/) removes most of the easy points beginners give away.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it normal to keep making the same mistake even after learning about it?

Yes, especially under time pressure or against a stronger opponent. Recognising a mistake in a book is easier than catching it live — that gap closes with practice and honest post-game review, not with reading alone.

### Should I focus on avoiding mistakes or on finding good moves?

Both matter, but at the beginner stage, cutting out blunders usually improves your results faster than finding brilliant moves, simply because most beginner games are decided by who blunders first.
