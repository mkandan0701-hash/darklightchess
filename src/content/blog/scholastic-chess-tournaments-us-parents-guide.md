**Short answer:** scholastic chess tournaments are events for students in kindergarten through grade 12, usually split into sections by grade or rating so children play others at a similar level. Many are rated by US Chess, which needs a US Chess membership. A first event typically has four to six rounds in one day, with prizes for top scorers in each section.

## What is a scholastic chess tournament?

A scholastic tournament is open only to school-age players. They range from small one-day events at a local school or club to state championships and national events. Children play every round against a new opponent, and their score (1 point for a win, ½ for a draw) decides the standings.

## How are sections organized?

Most events divide players so the games are fair:

- **By grade:** for example K–3, K–6, K–8 and K–12 sections.
- **By rating:** for example "Under 800" or "Under 1200", so newer players meet each other.
- **Unrated or novice sections:** for children playing their first rated games.

Check the event flyer: the sections, time control and prizes are listed there.

## Does my child need a US Chess membership?

For US Chess-rated events, yes. Each player needs a current US Chess membership, and their games count toward an official rating. A child's first rated games give them a provisional rating that settles as they play more. Some local events are unrated and don't need a membership. Our guide to [US Chess ratings for parents](/blog/us-chess-ratings-explained-for-parents/) explains how ratings work.

## Which tournaments are rated by US Chess?

Many school, club and state events are. US Chess also runs national scholastic championships for elementary, junior high and high school players, and a K–12 grade championship. Most children start with local events and only enter national ones after some experience. Your state chess association and local clubs list upcoming events.

## What happens on tournament day?

1. **Check-in:** confirm your child's registration and section.
2. **Pairings:** each round, a list shows who plays whom, at which board and with which color.
3. **Games:** players shake hands, start the clock and play. Parents usually wait outside the playing room.
4. **Between rounds:** time for food, water, a short rest and, ideally, a chat about something other than chess.
5. **Prizes:** awarded after the last round.

## What rules should my child know first?

- **Touch-move:** if you touch one of your pieces, you must move it if you legally can.
- **The clock:** press it with the same hand you moved with, and keep an eye on time.
- **Recording moves:** in longer games, players are usually expected to write down their moves. Many scholastic events relax this for the youngest sections, so check the rules.
- **Asking for help:** raise a hand for the tournament director; never ask a parent or coach during the game.

## How should a child prepare?

- Play practice games with a clock for a few weeks before.
- Practice writing moves in notation.
- Focus on tactics and simple endgames rather than new openings.
- Get a good night's sleep and pack snacks and water.

Afterwards, review the games with a coach or parent. The lesson from a loss is often worth more than the result. Read [how tournaments build your child's confidence](/blog/how-tournaments-build-your-childs-confidence/) for the parent's side.

## Frequently asked questions

### What age can children play scholastic tournaments?
Kindergarten students can enter K–3 or similar sections. Readiness matters more than age: a child who can play a full game alone and handle a loss is usually ready.

### How long does a scholastic tournament last?
A local one-day event usually runs from morning to mid-afternoon. Larger championships can run over two or three days.

### Can my child play if they've only just learned?
Yes. Novice or unrated sections exist for exactly that. A low-pressure first event is the best start.
