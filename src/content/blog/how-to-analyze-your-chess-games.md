Ask most improving players how they review their games, and the answer is usually the same: run it through an engine, look at the red arrows, note where the evaluation dropped, move on. It's fast, and it tells you *that* a move was a mistake — but it rarely tells you *why* you played it, which is the part that actually changes how you play next time. Before you open an engine at all, there's a more useful first pass you can do with just your own understanding.

## Why your own analysis should come first

An engine will happily tell you that move 23 lost two pawns. What it won't tell you is that you played that move because you'd misjudged your opponent's plan four moves earlier, or because you were low on time, or because you'd simply stopped looking for your opponent's ideas and were only calculating your own. Those are the patterns worth fixing, and you can only find them by reconstructing your own thinking — something no engine has access to.

Save the engine for the end, as a check on your conclusions, not as the first thing you reach for.

## Step 1: Replay the game without notes, from memory first

Set up the board and play through your own game slowly, ideally within a day or two while it's still fresh. Before looking at any notes or evaluations, try to remember what you were thinking at each of your own moves — not what you think now, in hindsight, but what your actual plan was at the time. This matters because the goal isn't to judge the move with today's clearer head; it's to find out where your in-game thinking went wrong.

## Step 2: Mark the moment the position changed

Every game has a handful of genuine turning points — moments where the balance of the position shifted, not because of a single blunder necessarily, but because a plan succeeded, failed, or was abandoned. Go through the game and mark two or three of these moments. Ask, at each one:

- What was I trying to achieve with my last few moves?
- What was my opponent trying to achieve, and did I notice it?
- Did the position change because of something I did, or something I missed?

This is more useful than marking every inaccuracy, because it forces you to think in terms of plans rather than isolated moves — which is closer to how chess is actually played.

## Step 3: Question your own plan, not just your moves

At each turning point, instead of just asking "was this the best move," ask "was this even the right idea." A move can be a reasonable execution of a bad plan, and no amount of calculation practice fixes a flawed plan — only recognising it does. Common patterns worth watching for in your own games:

- Continuing a plan after the position no longer supports it
- Reacting only to your opponent's last move instead of their overall idea
- Playing a move because it "felt natural" rather than because you checked it against a concrete threat

Writing these down in your own words, even briefly, makes them far more likely to stick than reading an engine's evaluation bar.

## Step 4: Only now, bring in an engine — to check, not to lead

Once you've formed your own view of what went wrong, run the game through an engine briefly. Use it to confirm or correct your own read of the critical moments, not to generate a fresh list of mistakes from scratch. If the engine flags a moment you'd already identified as a turning point, that's a good sign your own analysis is sharpening. If it flags something you missed entirely, that's useful too — but the point is you did the harder work of forming a judgement first.

This order matters especially for younger or newer players. Jumping straight to engine lines that are five moves deep and built on ideas well beyond their level teaches very little; it just produces a habit of nodding along to a number without understanding it.

## Step 5: Write one sentence per game, not a full report

You don't need pages of notes. After each analysed game, write a single sentence capturing the one pattern you want to carry into your next game — for example, "I stopped checking my opponent's plans once I felt I was ahead," or "I moved too fast in time trouble instead of taking the extra ten seconds." Collect these sentences across several games, and the recurring ones tell you exactly what to work on next.

## Do this for wins too

It's tempting to only analyse losses, but a won game often hides a shaky plan that worked anyway because your opponent made a bigger mistake. Applying the same process to wins — what was I actually trying to do, and did it hold up — prevents you from repeating a flawed idea just because it happened to succeed once.

## Make it a habit, not a one-off

A single deep review teaches you about one game. Doing this consistently, after every serious game you play, teaches you about yourself as a player — the specific habits and blind spots that show up again and again. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a chess engine to analyze my own games?

No — a chess engine is useful as a final check, but the most valuable part of analysis is reconstructing your own thinking and finding where your plan broke down, which an engine can't do for you.

### How soon after a game should I analyze it?

As soon as practically possible, ideally within a day or two, while your memory of your own thinking during the game is still accurate.

### Should I analyze my wins as well as my losses?

Yes. Wins can hide flawed plans that only worked because your opponent made a bigger error, so reviewing them the same way prevents you from repeating an idea that shouldn't have worked.

### How long should a game review take?

For most club-level games, twenty to thirty minutes of careful review is enough to find the key turning points and write down what you learned, especially once you're used to the process.
