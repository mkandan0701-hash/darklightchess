Ask five different players for "the best opening" and you'll get five different answers, mostly full of moves that take years to memorise properly. That's the wrong place for a beginner to start. What actually helps is a small set of openings built on sound ideas, easy to remember, and forgiving if your opponent doesn't play the "book" reply. Here are four that fit that description — two for White, two for Black — along with why they work, not just what to play.

If you haven't already, it's worth reading our piece on [opening principles every beginner should master](/blog/5-opening-principles-every-beginner-should-master/) first — the openings below are simply these principles applied in a specific order.

## For White

### The Italian Game

**Moves:** 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4

This is one of the oldest openings in chess, and still one of the best ways to learn good habits. You grab the centre with your e-pawn, develop your knight to attack it, then bring your bishop to its most active diagonal, aiming straight at Black's weakest point — the f7 square. From here you castle quickly, often play d3 or d4 to open lines for your pieces, and look for tactics against f7 or in the centre. Because the plan is so natural, you'll find yourself making good moves even when your opponent plays something unfamiliar.

### The London System

**Moves:** 1.d4 followed by Nf3, Bf4, e3, Bd3 (or Be2), c3, Nbd2 and castling kingside

The London's biggest selling point for beginners is that you can play almost the same setup regardless of what Black does. Get your bishop to f4 before playing e3, so it isn't trapped behind your own pawn, then fill in the rest of the setup and castle. It's solid, hard to attack early, and it means you spend your energy learning how to plan in the middlegame instead of memorising twenty different move orders.

## For Black

### The Caro-Kann Defence (against 1.e4)

**Moves:** 1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5

Instead of meeting 1.e4 with an immediate pawn to e5, the Caro-Kann prepares ...d5 first, so that after White's pawn takes or advances, Black's pawn structure stays solid and easy to understand. A common follow-up is developing the light-squared bishop to f5 or g4 before playing ...e6, since once that pawn goes to e6 the bishop would be stuck behind it. The resulting positions are calm and rarely punish a small mistake severely — ideal while you're still learning to spot tactics.

### The Queen's Gambit Declined (against 1.d4)

**Moves:** 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6

White's 2.c4 offers a pawn, but taking it (2...dxc4) leads to sharp positions that are hard for a beginner to handle accurately. Declining it with 2...e6 instead keeps your central pawn on d5, opens a diagonal for your dark-squared bishop, and sets you up for straightforward development with ...Nf6, ...Be7 and castling. It's the same opening world champions have relied on for over a century, precisely because it doesn't ask you to calculate anything scary in the first few moves.

## Why These Four and Not Others

Every opening above shares three things: they get a pawn into the centre early, they develop a knight and a bishop before anything fancy, and they lead to positions where a small mistake by you isn't immediately fatal. That last point matters more than most opening books admit — as a beginner, you will misstep sometimes, and the right opening should give you room to recover rather than punishing you on move eight.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I memorise more moves beyond what's shown here?

Not yet. Learn the ideas behind each move first — why the bishop goes to that square, why you castle when you do. Memorised moves without understanding fall apart the moment your opponent deviates.

### What if my opponent doesn't play the moves I expect?

That's normal, especially against other beginners. Stick to the same principles — centre, development, king safety — and you'll usually still reach a comfortable position even off the "main line."

### Do I need a different opening for online blitz games?

No. These same openings work at any time control. Faster games simply reward knowing your first several moves quickly, which repetition takes care of naturally.
