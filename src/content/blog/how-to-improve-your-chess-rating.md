Every player wants their rating to go up, and most players study more or less the same things: openings, tactics, maybe a few endgames. Yet ratings still plateau, often for years at a time. The gap usually isn't talent or effort — it's that the wrong things are being practiced, or the right things are being practiced without enough structure. Here's what actually moves a rating.

## Understand what a rating actually measures

A rating is not a measure of how much chess you know. It's a measure of how well you convert what you know into results, under real time pressure, against real opponents, move after move, for an entire game. This is why a player who has memorised twenty opening lines can still lose to someone with far less theoretical knowledge but steadier decision-making. If your rating feels stuck despite plenty of study, the issue is often conversion, not knowledge.

## Fix blunders before you add more openings

This is the single most common mistake among improving players: adding more opening theory while ignoring the blunders that are actually costing games. Go back through your last ten rated games and count how many were decided by a straightforward one- or two-move tactical oversight rather than a deep strategic error. For most players below a strong intermediate level, it's the majority of losses.

Tactical training fixes this faster than almost anything else. Fifteen minutes of puzzles a day, done consistently over months, builds the pattern recognition that prevents these blunders — far more efficiently than the same time spent memorising a new opening.

## Play longer time controls, not just fast ones

Blitz and bullet are fun and build some pattern recognition, but they train exactly the habit you don't want in classical or tournament games: quick, low-reflection decisions. If your rating goal is tied to classical or standard-rated events, most of your practice games should be played at a similar time control, even if that means fewer games per week. A player who plays fifty rushed blitz games a week often improves their rating slower than one who plays five slow, carefully considered games.

## Review your own games — properly

Most players glance over a lost game, notice the moment they blundered a piece, and move on. That's not a real review. A proper review means going through the whole game and asking, at each critical juncture, what your plan was and whether it held up — not just spotting the tactical error at the end. We've written a full method for doing this in our guide on [analysing your own games](/blog/how-to-analyze-your-chess-games/), and it's worth doing after every serious game you play, win or lose.

## Study endgames earlier than feels necessary

Endgames feel less exciting than tactics or openings, so most improving players postpone them. This is a mistake, because a large share of games at club and school level are decided in simplified positions where basic technique — king activity, opposition, simple rook endgames — makes the difference between a win and a draw, or a draw and a loss. A modest amount of endgame study returns more rating points per hour than almost any other category of work, because so few opponents at improving levels have studied it properly either.

## Keep your opening repertoire narrow and deep

You don't need ten openings — you need two or three that you understand well enough to navigate confidently past the point where your memorised moves run out. A narrow repertoire, studied deeply enough that you understand the resulting middlegame plans, will outperform a wide repertoire learned only a few moves deep. When you do lose in the opening phase, that's the moment to add or adjust a line — not before.

## Set process goals, not just rating goals

"Gain 200 points this year" is a fine long-term target, but it's not something you can act on day to day. Process goals — "review every tournament game within a week," "solve tactics for fifteen minutes daily," "play one slow game a week and analyse it fully" — are things you can actually control, and they're what produces the rating gain, not the other way around.

## Get outside feedback on your games

It's genuinely difficult to see your own recurring mistakes, because the same blind spots that cause an error in the game also make it hard to notice in review. A coach or a significantly stronger player looking at your games regularly will often spot patterns — a specific type of position you consistently misjudge, a time-management habit, a piece you routinely underdevelop — that take much longer to find on your own. ## Give it time

Rating improvement is rarely linear. Most players see plateaus lasting weeks or months, followed by a jump once a particular weakness has genuinely been fixed rather than just studied. Consistency across months matters more than intensity across days.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much should I study each day to improve my rating?

Thirty to forty-five minutes of focused, consistent daily practice — split between tactics, a slow game, or a review — tends to produce better results than occasional long weekend sessions.

### Why is my rating not improving even though I study every day?

Check what you're actually spending time on. Many plateaued players are studying openings or watching content passively rather than doing focused tactics, playing slow games, or reviewing their own play — the three activities that most directly affect results.

### Do I need a coach to improve my rating?

Not strictly, but a coach helps you find blind spots in your own games far faster than self-study alone, especially once you've made the easy early gains.

### How long does it typically take to see a rating jump?

This varies widely, but plateaus of a few months followed by a jump are common. Track process goals rather than the rating number week to week to avoid discouragement.
