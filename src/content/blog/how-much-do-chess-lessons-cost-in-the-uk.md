## What do UK chess tutors actually charge?

Prices vary mostly by the coach's level and by whether the lesson is one-to-one or in a group. These are advertised rates we found on public UK listings:

| Where | Typical advertised rate |
|---|---|
| Tutor marketplaces (for example [Findtutors](https://www.findtutors.co.uk/chess-tutors-/)) | About £10 an hour for some children's classes, many coaches between £15 and £35, and £75 or more for former national champions |
| Tutor guides such as [Tutorful](https://tutorful.co.uk/search/chess) | Private tutors quoted at roughly £30 to £50 an hour |
| Titled coaches listed on [Lichess](https://lichess.org/coach) | Roughly £40 an hour for some titled players, and £50 or more for grandmasters |

These are advertised prices, and individual coaches change them. Online lessons are often a little cheaper than in-person lessons because there is no travel.

## Why is there such a wide range?

Four things move the price most.

1. **The coach's playing level.** A grandmaster or international master usually charges more than a club-level coach. That is not always better value for a beginner, who needs clear teaching more than elite theory.
2. **One-to-one or group.** A private lesson costs more per hour than a place in a small group.
3. **Experience with children.** Teaching a seven-year-old well is a different skill from playing well.
4. **What is included.** Some fees cover only the lesson. Others include homework, game review and progress updates.

## Is a cheaper lesson worse?

Not automatically. For a child learning the rules and basic tactics, the most useful things are a live coach who corrects mistakes, a structured order of topics and regular practice. A high price does not guarantee those. Ask any coach or academy:

- Is the lesson live, with the coach watching my child play?
- Is there a curriculum, or does each lesson start from scratch?
- Who exactly will teach my child, and what is their record?
- Is there a free trial?

## What other costs should UK parents expect?

Lessons are only part of the budget. If your child starts playing tournaments, add:

- **Entry fees** for events and congresses, which vary by organiser.
- **ECF membership**, if you want to play in larger events. According to the [English Chess Federation](https://www.englishchess.org.uk/ecf-membership-rates-and-joining-details/), new juniors can get their first year of Bronze or Gold membership free, but you must complete the sign-up.
- **Travel and time** for weekend events.

None of this is needed at the start. Many children have lessons for months before their first tournament.

## How many lessons does a child need?

Once a week is a sensible start for most children, with a little practice between lessons. More frequent lessons cost more, so it is worth deciding what you want first. A child who simply enjoys the game needs less than one preparing for a junior event.

## How to compare prices fairly

1. Ask for the price per hour, not per package.
2. Check the currency and whether any fees are added.
3. Take the free trial lesson wherever it is offered, and judge the teaching, not the price.
4. Check the lesson times fit your child's school day. Our timings are flexible, and India is 5½ hours ahead of the UK in winter and 4½ hours ahead in summer, so after-school and weekend slots are available.
