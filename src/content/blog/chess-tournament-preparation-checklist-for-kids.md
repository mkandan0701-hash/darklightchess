Most of the stress around a child's first few tournaments has nothing to do with chess. It's the missing pencil, the forgotten entry confirmation, the scramble to find the venue with ten minutes to spare. A little preparation the night before removes almost all of it, leaving your child free to focus on the actual games. Here's a practical checklist we share with parents at our academy.

## The night before: confirm, don't assume

Do these the evening before the event, not the morning of:

- Confirm the reporting time and round-one start time — these are often different.
- Check the venue address and how long the journey will actually take, including parking or drop-off.
- Verify your child's registration is confirmed and note their board number or registration ID if it's already published.
- Check the tournament's format: number of rounds, time control, and whether it runs across one day or several.
- Lay out clothes and the tournament bag so the morning isn't rushed.

A rushed morning raises a child's stress before they've even sat down at the board, and that nervous energy tends to carry into the first game.

## What to pack: the bag itself

A dedicated tournament bag, packed the night before, saves you from last-minute searching. Include:

- **Pen or pencil**, and a spare — scoresheets get filled in by hand, and pencils go missing constantly.
- **A chess set and clock**, if the tournament asks players to bring their own (many school and academy-level events do; larger open tournaments usually provide boards).
- **Water bottle and a light snack** — dried fruit, biscuits, or a sandwich that won't spill.
- **A cardigan or light jacket** — tournament halls are often air-conditioned and can get cold for hours of sitting still.
- **Entry confirmation or ID**, printed or saved on a phone, in case registration needs to be verified at the desk.
- **A book, puzzle sheet, or quiet activity** for the gaps between rounds, which can stretch longer than expected.

## What to check on the morning of

- Eat a proper breakfast — not a heavy one, but enough that hunger doesn't become a distraction two hours into a game.
- Leave with enough buffer time to find parking, locate the right hall, and settle in before the round starts. Arriving five minutes before a round begins is far more stressful than arriving thirty minutes early.
- Do a final tactics warm-up if your child's coach recommends one — five or ten quick puzzles, nothing new or difficult, just to get the brain moving.

## What to bring for you, the parent

Waiting rooms at chess tournaments can mean several hours between rounds, especially at multi-round events. Bring something to occupy yourself — most halls do not allow parents to watch play from close range, and for good reason: a child playing under a parent's gaze often plays more anxiously, not less. For more on what to expect from the parent's side specifically, see our guide on [what parents should expect at a child's first tournament](/blog/what-parents-should-expect-at-their-childs-first-chess-tournament/).

## During the tournament: keep it simple between rounds

Between games, avoid replaying the previous round in detail with your child, especially if they lost. A simple "well played, get some water" does more good than an on-the-spot post-mortem. There will be time to review the game properly once you're home — for a method that actually helps a child learn from their own games rather than just hearing what went wrong, see our piece on [analysing your own chess games](/blog/how-to-analyze-your-chess-games/).

## After the tournament: the follow-up checklist

- Collect the scoresheets — coaches use these for review, and some rating systems require them.
- Note the final standings or rating change, if published, for your records.
- Ask your child what they found hardest, rather than only asking what the result was.
- Bring the games to your next coaching session for review while they're still fresh.

## A checklist is preparation, not pressure

The point of a checklist like this isn't to add more things for a child to worry about — it's the opposite. When the logistics are handled in advance, a child walks into the hall with one less thing pulling at their attention, and that mental space goes straight into the games. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does my child need to bring their own chess set to a tournament?

It depends on the event. Many school and academy-level tournaments ask players to bring their own set and clock; larger rated or open tournaments usually provide boards and clocks at each table. Always check the tournament notice in advance.

### How early should we arrive on the day of a tournament?

Aim to arrive at least 30 minutes before the reporting time, not the round start time. This allows time to find the hall, confirm registration, and settle in calmly.

### What snacks are appropriate to bring into a tournament hall?

Quiet, low-mess snacks are best — dried fruit, biscuits, or a sealed sandwich. Avoid anything crunchy or with strong packaging noise, since many halls expect silence during play.

### Can parents watch their child play during a round?

Most tournaments restrict how closely parents can observe play, and many don't allow it at all near the board. This is standard practice, not a sign of anything wrong — it helps children focus and reduces performance pressure.
