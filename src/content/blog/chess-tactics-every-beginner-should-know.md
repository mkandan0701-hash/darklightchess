Strategy tells you what to aim for over the whole game; tactics are the short, forcing sequences that actually win material or deliver checkmate along the way. Almost every decisive game at beginner and club level is settled by one side spotting a tactic the other missed. These are the patterns worth drilling first.

## The Fork

A fork is when one piece attacks two or more enemy pieces at the same time, and the opponent can only save one of them. Knights are the classic forking piece because their attacking squares are so easy to miss — a knight landing on a square where it simultaneously threatens a king and a rook, for instance, wins material almost automatically since the king must move out of check first. Pawns, bishops, rooks and queens can all fork too; the pattern to look for is simply two undefended (or under-defended) targets a single move away.

## The Pin

A pin happens when a piece can't move — or shouldn't move — because doing so would expose a more valuable piece behind it on the same line. The clearest example appears in one of the oldest openings in chess: after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5, White's bishop pins Black's knight on c6 to the king on e8. That's an **absolute pin** — the knight legally cannot move at all, since moving it would leave the king in check. A **relative pin** is similar but the piece behind isn't the king, just something valuable like a queen; moving the pinned piece is legal, but usually costly.

## The Skewer

A skewer is a pin in reverse. Instead of attacking a lesser piece to expose a bigger one behind it, you attack the more valuable piece first, forcing it to move out of the way, and then capture the less valuable piece that was hiding behind it. A common version is a rook or bishop checking the enemy king along a rank, file or diagonal, with another enemy piece sitting right behind it on the same line — once the king steps aside, that piece falls.

## The Discovered Attack

This occurs when you move one piece out of the way of another piece behind it, revealing an attack that piece was blocking. The piece that moves can make its own threat too, which is what makes discovered attacks so dangerous — your opponent may have to deal with two threats from a single move. When the revealed attack is a check on the enemy king, it's called a **discovered check**, and it's one of the most powerful tactical devices in chess because the opponent has no choice but to address the check first, even if the moving piece is capturing something valuable elsewhere.

## Removing the Defender

Sometimes a piece is only safe because another piece is protecting it. If you can capture, deflect, or otherwise eliminate that defender — even by giving up material to do it — the piece it was protecting often becomes winnable. This idea, sometimes called deflection, is behind many combinations that look like sacrifices at first glance but are actually calculated exactly.

## Back-Rank Weaknesses

A king that has castled behind its own pawns on the second or seventh rank, with no pawn moved in front of it, can be checkmated by a single rook or queen reaching the back rank — the king simply has nowhere to run. This is called a back-rank mate, and it catches out surprisingly strong players, not just beginners. The fix is simple: at some point in the middlegame, create a little "luft" (breathing room) by moving one pawn in front of your king, so it always has an escape square if a rook or queen arrives on the back rank.

## How to Actually Get Good at Spotting These

Reading the definitions above is the easy part — recognising these patterns in your own games, under time pressure, takes repetition. The most effective way we've seen is solving short tactical puzzles daily, ideally ones built around a single theme at a time (all forks one day, all pins the next) rather than random mixed puzzles from the start. It's also worth reviewing your own losses specifically to check whether a fork, pin or hanging piece decided the game — that tells you exactly which pattern to drill next. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which tactic should I learn first?

The fork, since it's the easiest to spot and appears constantly even in beginner games — especially knight forks.

### Do I need to memorise famous tactical combinations?

Not really. What matters is recognising the underlying pattern — a fork looks different in every game, but the shape (two targets, one attacker) is always the same.

### How many tactics puzzles should a beginner solve per day?

Even ten to fifteen minutes a day, done consistently, builds pattern recognition faster than an occasional long session once a week.
