**Short answer:** neither format is better for every child. Private lessons follow one child's pace and fix their specific mistakes, so they suit fast improvers, children who are stuck, and tournament players. Group classes cost less, give children practice partners and keep beginners motivated. Many families start in a group and add private lessons before tournaments.

## Side by side

| | Private (1-to-1) | Small group |
|---|---|---|
| Pace | Set entirely by your child | Set for the group's level |
| Feedback | On every move your child makes | Shared between students |
| Practice partners | The coach | Other children at the same level |
| Motivation | Coach attention | Friendly competition and friends |
| Scheduling | Most flexible | Fixed weekly slot |
| Cost per hour | Higher | Lower |

## Who private lessons suit

- **Children far ahead of their age group**, who get bored waiting for others.
- **Children who are stuck**, repeating the same mistakes or losing confidence.
- **Tournament players** preparing for a specific event, who need their own games reviewed.
- **Shy children**, who may ask more questions without an audience.

## Who group classes suit

- **Most beginners**, who learn well from seeing other children make and fix the same mistakes.
- **Children who love playing**, since a group gives them opponents at their level every week.
- **Families on a budget**, who want regular lessons at a lower cost.

## What about siblings?

Brothers and sisters at a similar level can share a sibling class: more personal than a group and cheaper than two private lessons. If their levels are far apart, separate classes work better. At Darklight, families with more than one child get a sibling discount.

## Combining both

A common pattern is a weekly group class for steady progress, plus a private session every few weeks or before a tournament to review the child's own games. Our [tournament coaching](/tournament-chess-coaching/) works this way.

## How to decide

1. **Start with a level assessment.** At Darklight the free demo includes one, and the coach will recommend a format.
2. **Think about your goal.** Enjoyment and steady learning usually suit a group. A specific tournament or rating goal often needs private time.
3. **Review after a month.** It's easy to switch formats once you see how your child responds.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are private chess lessons worth the extra cost?
They are when your child needs individual attention, such as before tournaments or when they're stuck. For many beginners, a group is better value.

### How many children are in a group?
It varies by provider, so ask. Small groups give each child more of the coach's attention.

### Can my child switch from group to private later?
Yes. Most academies, including ours, let you change format as your child's needs change.
